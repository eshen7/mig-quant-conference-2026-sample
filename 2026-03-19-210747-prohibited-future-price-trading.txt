❯ Prohibited — Future Price Trading                                             
                                                                                
Your get_actions function receives the full price matrix at once, meaning       
future prices are technically present in the array. You must not use any price  
at index t when deciding the action for a day earlier than t. Strategies that   
look ahead in time — directly or via derived features — constitute future price 
 trading and are disqualified.                                                  
                                                                                
Examples of violations: using prices[i, t+1] in day-t decisions, sorting or     
ranking stocks by future returns, or training a model on out-of-sample future   
labels.. does strategy_combined break this rule?                                

  Searched for 1 pattern, read 1 file (ctrl+o to expand)

⏺ Yes, strategy_combined has look-ahead bias. Both sub-strategies share the same
   issue:

  The violation

  Parameters are estimated on days 0 to train_end (85% of data), but trading 
  starts on day 1 — well before train_end. This means:

  1. spread_mean (lines 98, 231) is computed from spreads[:train_end]. On day 5,
   for example, the strategy already knows the average spread through day
  ~0.85*num_days — that's future information.
  2. Regression weights (lines 57, 189) are fit via OLS on train_prices[:, 
  :train_end]. When trading on day 10, the model already incorporates price
  relationships from days 11 through train_end.
  3. Correlation matrix (lines 35, 168) used for pair selection is computed over
   the full training window, giving early-day decisions knowledge of future
  co-movements.
  4. rolling_std in _adaptive_threshold (line 238) is fine by itself (it only
  looks backward), but it operates on centered_spreads which uses the
  forward-contaminated spread_mean.

  How to fix it

  Either:
  - Start trading at train_end — only generate non-zero actions for day >= 
  train_end, so all training data is strictly in the past.
  - Use an expanding window — on each day t, estimate parameters from prices[:, 
  :t] only (much slower but no look-ahead at all).

  The first option is simpler and keeps the current structure intact.

❯ can you think of ways i can minimize drawdown?                                

⏺ A few practical ideas given your current setup:

  1. Stop-losses per pair — Currently positions are only closed when the spread
  crosses the exit threshold. Add a hard stop if the spread moves e.g. 3x the
  entry threshold against you. This caps the worst-case loss per trade.
  2. Portfolio-level circuit breaker — Track cumulative PnL and stop opening new
   positions if drawdown exceeds a threshold (e.g., -$2,000). Resume only after
  recovery.
  3. Volatility-scaled position sizing — Instead of fixed max_position=20, scale
   inversely with recent volatility: position = min(max_position, target_risk / 
  rolling_vol). Smaller positions in turbulent markets.
  4. Tighter exits — Your current exit_threshold=0.0 (returns-based) means you
  hold until the spread fully reverts. A slightly positive exit threshold closes
   trades earlier, sacrificing some upside but cutting tail risk.
  5. Max gross exposure cap — Sum absolute positions across all stocks; if total
   exposure exceeds a limit (e.g., $15,000), skip new entries. Prevents
  overleverage when many pairs signal simultaneously.
  6. Pair correlation filter — Active pairs may be correlated (e.g., overlapping
   basket members), so losses pile up together. Filter out pairs whose spreads
  are highly correlated with already-active pairs.

  Want me to implement any of these? I'd suggest starting with stop-losses (#1)
  and a portfolio circuit breaker (#2) — they're the highest impact for the
  least complexity.

  Is ETF stat arb valid here?

  It can work, but the current implementation is heavily overfit because:

  1. Training on 85% = trading on training data. The in-sample PnL is inflated
  because spreads naturally mean-revert around parameters estimated from that
  same data.
  2. Static weights over 4 years. OLS cointegration relationships shift as
  market regimes change (COVID, rate hikes, tech rotation). Fixed weights from
  2017–2021 don't hold in 2021–2025.
  3. Small stock universe. 30 stocks means limited pair diversity — you're
  fitting noise.

  Ideas to reduce overfitting

  For stat arb specifically:
  - Regularize pair selection — require higher R² thresholds (0.90+), longer
  half-life of mean reversion, and ADF stationarity tests on the spread
  - Fewer parameters — simpler models overfit less. A 2-stock pair with one
  hedge ratio is more robust than a 4-stock basket with 5 OLS weights
  - Wider entry thresholds — only trade large spread dislocations (high
  conviction signals)

  For any strategy in this competition:
  - Walk-forward validation — the semi-sliding window test we ran earlier
  (-$16k) is the honest estimate. If it doesn't work walk-forward, it won't work
   on hidden test data
  - Reduce trade frequency — more trades = more opportunities to overfit. A
  strategy that makes 50 high-conviction trades is more robust than one making
  5,000
  - Simpler signals — momentum, mean reversion on single stocks, or sector
  rotation strategies have fewer parameters to overfit
  - Ensemble across regimes — instead of one fixed model, blend signals from
  multiple simple strategies

  Alternative strategy directions worth exploring:
  - Cross-sectional momentum — rank stocks by recent returns, go long winners /
  short losers. Only 1–2 parameters (lookback, holding period)
  - Simple mean reversion — Bollinger Bands or RSI on individual stocks. Very
  few parameters
  - Risk parity / volatility targeting — allocate inversely to volatility.
  Nearly parameter-free
